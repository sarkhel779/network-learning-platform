A monitor-mode-capable wireless adapter can capture a 4-way handshake directly off the air during a client's association — the basis for every WPA2 password-recovery tool, and for legitimate password-strength auditing on networks you're authorized to test.

<h2 id="guided-handshake-practice">Guided handshake practice</h2>

Work through why password length matters so much more for WPA2-Personal than it does for, say, an online login form. An online login form can lock an account after a handful of failed attempts, making brute-forcing impractical regardless of password length. A captured WPA2 handshake has no such limit: the attacker's guessing happens entirely on their own hardware, so the only real defense is making the search space itself infeasibly large — a long, high-entropy passphrase (16+ random characters, or a multi-word passphrase) resists this kind of attack even though the exact same captured handshake would be available to attack either way.

<InterviewScenario title="A company's WPA2-Personal Wi-Fi password is 8 characters, all lowercase letters">A candidate argues this is safe because WPA2 uses strong AES encryption. Explain why the encryption strength is irrelevant to this specific weakness: the vulnerability is in deriving the key from a low-entropy password via a handshake anyone can capture, not in the strength of the cipher used afterward — an attacker doesn't need to break AES at all, only to guess the password offline.</InterviewScenario>

<h2 id="troubleshoot-wifi-security">Troubleshoot Wi-Fi security</h2>

1. Confirm both the client and AP are actually configured for the same WPA version and cipher — a client that only supports WPA2 cannot connect to an AP configured for WPA3-only, and the resulting failure often looks like a wrong-password error instead of a compatibility mismatch.
2. Check for a "WPA3 transition mode" AP broadcasting both WPA2 and WPA3 simultaneously — while this maximizes compatibility, it reintroduces WPA2's offline dictionary-attack surface for any client (or attacker) that connects via the WPA2 side.
3. Rule out a simple typo or case-sensitivity issue in the password before assuming a security or configuration problem — Wi-Fi passwords are case-sensitive and this remains the most common cause of authentication failures.
4. If 802.1X/Enterprise authentication fails, check the RADIUS server's logs directly rather than the client, since Enterprise failures (expired certificates, misconfigured EAP methods) usually surface clearly server-side but only as a generic failure on the client.
5. Distinguish an authentication failure (wrong password or incompatible security settings, happens before any IP configuration) from a DHCP or IP-layer failure (associates and authenticates fine but can't get an address) — they point to completely different layers of the connection process.

<InterviewScenario title="A guest network was recently switched from WPA2 to WPA3, and a handful of older employee devices can no longer connect at all">Explain the most likely cause and the trade-off in the two ways to fix it: enabling WPA3 transition mode restores compatibility for the older devices but reopens WPA2's offline dictionary-attack surface on that network, while keeping WPA3-only forces those specific older devices to be replaced or moved to a separate, WPA2-only network instead.</InterviewScenario>
