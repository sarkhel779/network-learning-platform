<h2 id="inspect-association-evidence">Inspect association evidence</h2>

A monitor-mode wireless capture during a client joining a network shows the full scan-authenticate-associate sequence as separate, identifiable management frames — useful for confirming exactly where a slow or failed connection attempt is actually stalling.

Identify the Association Request (subtype 0x00), Association Response (subtype 0x01), and Authentication frames (subtype 0x0b), and note the Association ID the AP assigns in the Association Response.</WiresharkCheck>

<h2 id="guided-standards-practice">Guided standards practice</h2>

Work through why a seemingly simple purchase decision — "which Wi-Fi standard does this device support?" — actually determines real-world behavior on a mixed network. A network has an 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) access point serving a mix of Wi-Fi 6 laptops and several older 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4) IoT sensors. Because 802.11 is backward compatible, the older devices can still associate and communicate — but every legacy device on the channel can force the AP to fall back to older, slower protection mechanisms for traffic timing to remain compatible, reducing the effective throughput available even to the Wi-Fi 6 clients sharing the same channel.

<InterviewScenario title="A newly purchased 'Wi-Fi 6' router doesn't seem any faster than the old one it replaced">A candidate assumes the new router is defective. Explain a more likely explanation to check first: the actual client devices connecting to it (laptops, phones) may not themselves support 802.11ax, in which case they'll negotiate down to whatever older standard they do support, and the router's new capabilities go largely unused regardless of how capable the router itself is.</InterviewScenario>

<h2 id="troubleshoot-association-issues">Troubleshoot association issues</h2>

1. Distinguish an association failure (the client never reaches State 3 at all — check for an AP client-count limit, a MAC filter, or a band/standard mismatch) from an authentication failure after association (reaches State 3 but the WPA2/WPA3 handshake fails — almost always a wrong password or an incompatible security mode).
2. Check the AP's maximum associated-client count before assuming a configuration problem — many consumer APs silently refuse new associations once a client limit is reached, with no obvious error shown to the user.
3. If a client repeatedly scans but never even attempts authentication, confirm it actually received a Probe Response or Beacon for that SSID — a hidden SSID (one that suppresses Beacons) requires the client to actively probe for the exact name, and a typo there looks identical to "network not found."
4. For a device that works near the AP but fails to associate from farther away, consider that the client's Probe Request or Association Request frame may be too weak for the AP to decode reliably even though the AP's stronger Beacon is still audible to the client — association requires a two-way link budget, not just one-way audibility.
5. When roaming misbehaves (a client "sticks" to a weak, distant AP instead of roaming to a strong, nearby one with the same SSID), check whether 802.11k/v are enabled and supported by the client — without them, roaming is entirely the client's own decision, and many clients are deliberately conservative about giving up an existing association.

<InterviewScenario title="A client device stays connected to a distant, weak AP even while standing right next to a much stronger one with the same SSID, in an office with several APs">Explain why this is normal default behavior rather than a bug: the roaming decision belongs to the client, not the network, and without 802.11k (neighbor reports) and 802.11v (network-suggested transitions) to assist it, many client radios are tuned to avoid roaming until the signal drops quite low, to avoid unnecessarily interrupting an active connection.</InterviewScenario>
