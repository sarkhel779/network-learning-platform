<h2 id="design-a-connection" tabIndex={-1} aria-describedby="connection-design-context">Design a connection</h2>

<ConnectionMediaExperience progressItemId="cables_fibre_wireless_and_network_connections_interactive_design_a_connection" scenarios={loadAccountConnectionScenarios()} />

Choose a scenario, read every requirement, select a medium, then check your reasoning.
**Recommended** means the best overall fit for those requirements. **Workable with trade-offs**
means a compromise needs attention. **Unsuitable** means a decisive requirement is not met.
Changing the requirements can change the recommendation; these are not permanent rankings.

Start with the nearby home desktop, then compare the mobile office, noisy workshop, campus
buildings, high-capacity data-centre interconnect, and temporary classroom. For a compact fixed
home setup with a suitable cable path, installation cost and reliability matter more than
mobility. The recommended connection for a nearby desktop is copper Ethernet.
Campus buildings need a connection chosen for distance and environmental exposure.
Use the lab's actual requirements before applying either example elsewhere.

<h2 id="diagnose-link-symptoms">Diagnose link symptoms</h2>

Use this six-step loop to turn an observation into a testable explanation:

1. **Define the symptom and scope.** Record what fails, when it started, and whether one device,
   one location, or several users are affected. Separate no connection from slow or intermittent service.
2. **Check power, connection, and link state.** Confirm powered equipment and seated connections
   at both ends. A lit link indicator proves physical or link negotiation, not end-to-end connectivity.
3. **Compare speed and duplex where applicable.** Check both Ethernet endpoints and their
   expected capabilities. A negotiated rate is evidence about the link, not a speed-test guarantee.
4. **Inspect the path and environment.** Check cable length, damage, seating, and compatible
   fibre components; for wireless, compare signal quality, obstructions, location, and channel conditions.
5. **Change one variable.** Use an appropriate known-good cable or controlled location change.
   Avoid changing several settings at once, which hides the cause of any improvement.
6. **Retest and record.** Repeat the same test and note the before/after evidence. Escalate with
   those observations if the cause remains uncertain.

### Disconnected or damaged copper

No link or intermittent drops may follow a loose plug or damaged pair. Inspect both ends and
visible cable damage, then substitute a suitable known-good cable. A stable result after one
controlled substitution supports a cable-path fault; the symptom alone does not prove one.

### Excessive copper distance

A long installed channel can fail to negotiate or become unreliable. Measure the whole path,
including patch cords, and compare it with the chosen Ethernet mode's cabling requirements.
For the 1000BASE-T example, a channel beyond 100 metres is outside the stated supported scope.
Redesign the path or use a suitable alternative; a link light does not certify that installation.

### Speed or duplex mismatch

A lower-than-expected negotiated rate or errors under load warrants checking both endpoints'
capabilities, settings, and counters. A duplex mismatch can impair performance, but low throughput
alone does not prove it. Restore compatible negotiation/settings through the responsible
administrator and retest; do not force a setting on just one side as a guess.

### Incompatible fibre components

A fibre connector may fit while the link remains down. Compare the fibre type, transceiver
specifications, supported signalling, and transmit/receive path at both ends. Confirm seating
and follow approved cleaning/inspection procedures. A missing link indication alone cannot tell you
which component is wrong; compatibility is a separate check from physical fit.

### Weak Wi-Fi from distance or obstruction

A connection that worsens behind walls may have insufficient signal quality. Compare the same
device and task near the access point and at the affected location. Improvement supports a
coverage problem, but does not identify the exact obstruction or exclude other causes.
Adjust placement or coverage using the recorded locations and retest.

### Channel congestion or radio interference

Wi-Fi may slow down despite a strong signal when many devices compete for airtime or other
radio energy disrupts reception. Compare busy and quiet periods, affected locations, channel
utilization, and retry evidence where available. A low speed-test result cannot identify a
particular interfering device. Plan an authorized channel or placement change from evidence,
then repeat the same test.

<InterviewScenario prompt="A colleague says a lit Ethernet port proves the network is healthy. How would you respond?">

The light is evidence of a physical/link condition, not a completed application exchange.
I would establish which task fails and its scope, compare negotiated speed and duplex at both
ends, then test the task again after checking the path. I would report observed results and
remaining uncertainty, rather than declare the cable or the whole network healthy.

</InterviewScenario>

<InterviewScenario prompt="A user reports that Wi-Fi is slow. Can you conclude there is radio interference?">

No. Slow throughput is a symptom with several possible causes. I would compare signal quality,
location, busy and quiet periods, and available channel/retry evidence. A weak signal suggests
a coverage issue to investigate; a strong signal with busy airtime suggests a capacity issue
to investigate. Neither observation identifies an exact interference source by itself.

</InterviewScenario>
