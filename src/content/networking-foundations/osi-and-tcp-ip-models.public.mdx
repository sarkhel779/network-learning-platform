<h2 id="why-layers">Why layered models exist</h2>

Layered models divide networking work into smaller responsibilities. Each layer describes a useful group of tasks, making a complex system easier to learn, build, and discuss.

The models are guides rather than physical stacks of boxes. Real products may combine responsibilities while the concepts remain useful.

<h2 id="osi-model">The seven OSI layers</h2>

The **OSI model** has seven conceptual layers:

1. **Physical** — signals, media, and bits.
2. **Data Link** — local frames and link identifiers.
3. **Network** — logical addressing between networks.
4. **Transport** — end-to-end application conversations.
5. **Session** — organizing an application exchange.
6. **Presentation** — representation, compression, and encryption formats.
7. **Application** — services used by applications.

<h2 id="tcp-ip-model">The TCP/IP model</h2>

The practical **TCP/IP model** commonly groups responsibilities into four layers: Network Access, Internet, Transport, and Application. The name refers to the Internet protocol suite; it does not mean every exchange must use TCP.

<h2 id="model-mapping">OSI-to-TCP/IP mapping</h2>

TCP/IP Network Access roughly combines OSI Physical and Data Link. TCP/IP Internet aligns with OSI Network. Transport aligns with Transport. TCP/IP Application groups the OSI Session, Presentation, and Application responsibilities.

<h2 id="encapsulation-lab">Encapsulation and layer matching</h2>

When a sender prepares data, each layer supplies information needed for its responsibility. The receiver interprets those layers in the other direction. This process is called **encapsulation** and **decapsulation**.
