<h2 id="inspect-icmp-evidence">Inspect ICMP evidence</h2>

On **Windows**, start with `ping 192.0.2.44` and use `tracert 192.0.2.44` for its ICMP Echo based path evidence. On **Linux**, compare `ping 192.0.2.44`, `traceroute 192.0.2.44`, and `tracepath 192.0.2.44`. State which probe transport the command actually uses; platform and options can change it.

In Wireshark, begin with `icmp`, then narrow representative evidence with `icmp.type == 0`, `icmp.type == 3`, or `icmp.type == 11`. Correlate identifiers, sequence numbers, source and destination IPv4 addresses, reporter address, type/code, checksum status, and quoted trigger fields. A missing response remains absence of observed evidence, not proof of one failure cause.

<WiresharkCheck displayFilter="icmp">Capture one Echo exchange or ICMP error. Identify the request, intended destination, reporting device, type/code, and quoted packet evidence before writing a conclusion.</WiresharkCheck>

<h2 id="guided-icmp-diagnosis">Guided ICMP diagnosis</h2>

For each of the six ping outcomes—success, timeout, network unreachable, host unreachable, administratively prohibited, and TTL exceeded—separate what the source sent from what it observed. If an error arrived, identify the reporter and its quoted packet. If nothing arrived, preserve that uncertainty.

<h2 id="troubleshoot-icmp">Troubleshoot ICMP methodically</h2>

1. Verify source and destination **addressing**, including the prefix and mask.
2. Verify the selected **route** to the intended destination.
3. Verify the local **gateway** and its Layer 2 resolution when the destination is off-link.
4. Check host firewall and other **local policy** affecting the request or returning evidence.
5. Check network and destination **remote policy**, including filtering and rate limiting.
6. Investigate the **return path** separately; it may differ from the forward path.
7. Test **application reachability** independently because successful Echo is not a service-port test.
