Every network connection carries bits by changing a physical signal. Copper uses electrical
changes, fibre uses light, and wireless uses radio. Each can be a good choice when it matches
the distance, environment, capacity, and mobility a connection needs.

<h2 id="how-connections-carry-data">How connections carry data</h2>

Devices turn data into signals that a receiving interface can recognize and turn back into bits.
The signal changes quickly, but the useful question is simple: which medium can carry a reliable
signal across this particular connection?

<h2 id="connection-qualities">Connection qualities</h2>

Bandwidth is the theoretical carrying capacity, while throughput is the useful rate actually
achieved. Latency is delay. Distance, interference, reliability, mobility, cost, speed, duplex,
and link state also influence whether a connection is a good fit.

<h2 id="copper-ethernet">Copper Ethernet</h2>

Twisted-pair Ethernet commonly uses copper conductors and RJ45 connectors. It is practical for
nearby fixed devices, but common twisted-pair Ethernet channels have a 100-metre limit and can
be affected by electromagnetic interference or poor installation.

<h2 id="fibre-connections">Fibre connections</h2>

Fibre carries light through glass or plastic strands. It can suit long distances and high
capacity, but connectors, transceivers, handling, compatibility, and installation costs matter
when comparing it with other media.

<h2 id="wireless-connections">Wireless connections</h2>

Wireless carries radio signals through a shared environment. It supports mobility, while
distance, obstacles, competing signals, and shared airtime can affect the connection.

<h2 id="compare-media">Compare connection media</h2>

Copper, fibre, and wireless are trade-offs rather than a ranking. Compare their distance,
capacity, resistance to interference, mobility, and cost before choosing one for a connection.
