<h2 id="inspect-transport-evidence">Inspect TCP evidence</h2>

Filter a capture with `tcp`. Inspect `tcp.flags.syn == 1` for opening attempts and `tcp.analysis.retransmission` as a lead, then verify sequence ranges and ACKs yourself. On Linux, compare the capture with `ss -tulpen`; on Windows, use `Get-NetTCPConnection` or `netstat -ano`.

<WiresharkCheck displayFilter="tcp">Capture one TCP exchange. Record the endpoint tuple, MSS and Window Scale offered in SYN packets, advertised receive windows, sequence ranges, cumulative ACKs, any SACK blocks, and the observed recovery path.</WiresharkCheck>

<h2 id="guided-transport-diagnosis">Guided TCP diagnosis</h2>

A completed handshake proves transport setup, not application success. Repeated SYNs without a reply indicate an incomplete attempt but not which direction or policy caused the silence. Three duplicate ACKs can support fast retransmit; a single duplicate ACK does not establish that trigger.

<InterviewScenario title="A download pauses after one lost segment">Identify the missing byte range, cumulative ACK, SACK block edges, duplicate ACK count, and whether recovery used fast retransmit or a timer. Keep receiver-window flow control separate from congestion control.</InterviewScenario>

<h2 id="troubleshoot-transport">Troubleshoot TCP methodically</h2>

1. Verify the address, port, listener, and return path.
2. Capture SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK, payload, and closing packets in both directions.
3. Check MSS and Window Scale only where negotiated, in SYN packets.
4. Compare cumulative ACKs and SACK blocks to find missing versus buffered bytes.
5. Distinguish RST, fast retransmit, timeout retransmit, and application-level errors.
